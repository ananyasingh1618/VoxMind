Photosynthesis in plants proceeds through two linked stages. The light-dependent reactions occur across the thylakoid membrane inside the chloroplast, where chlorophyll and accessory pigments absorb photons and use that energy to split water molecules, releasing oxygen as a byproduct. The excited electrons travel down an electron transport chain, pumping protons into the thylakoid lumen and ultimately generating ATP and NADPH, the two energy-carrying molecules the cell needs for the next stage.

The light-independent reactions, commonly called the Calvin cycle, take place in the stroma surrounding the thylakoids and do not directly require light. Here, the enzyme RuBisCO fixes atmospheric carbon dioxide onto a five-carbon sugar, and the ATP and NADPH produced earlier drive a series of reduction steps that ultimately regenerate the starting sugar and produce glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, the molecule plants use to build glucose and other carbohydrates.
